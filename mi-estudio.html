Context

An accounting tool built for accountants, but not for their clients

Mi Estudio is a B2B accounting management platform used by accounting firms to manage client portfolios, documents, deadlines, and billing. The product had grown organically and accumulated structural complexity: features added over time without a coherent information architecture to hold them together.

Accountants could navigate it because they'd learned its quirks. Their clients couldn't, and the friction was creating support burden and slowing onboarding.

Problem

Two users, one interface not serving either

The platform served two very different users: accountants, who needed power and efficiency across large client portfolios; and clients, who needed clarity about their own situation without being overwhelmed by the full product surface.

The existing interface blurred the boundary between these two roles, surfacing accountant-specific features to clients and burying client-relevant information under professional tooling.

The core tension

Design a unified product that gives accountants the depth they need for portfolio management while giving clients a clear, simple window into their own account, without maintaining two entirely separate products.

My role

Full-cycle design ownership, from discovery to system

I owned this project end-to-end across research, architecture, and design:

  • User research with both accountants and clients to understand workflow differences
  • Information architecture redesign: restructuring navigation and content hierarchy for both roles
  • Dashboard design optimised for portfolio scanning (accountant) and status clarity (client)
  • Document management flow redesign: reducing the steps to find, upload, and share files
  • Full design system delivery for consistent future development

Process

Starting with the information architecture

The first phase was audit and research: mapping the existing IA, identifying where users got lost, and documenting the workflow differences between accountants and clients through interviews and session observation.

The finding that drove everything: accountants think in portfolios (across clients), clients think in tasks (their own pending actions). The navigation needed to reflect this, not flatten it.

Key design decisions

Designing for two mental models

Decision 01

Role-based dashboard views

Rather than a single dashboard trying to serve both users, we designed distinct home screens — the accountant view leading with portfolio status across clients, the client view leading with pending tasks and recent documents.

Decision 02

File-first navigation

Documents are the primary unit of work in accounting. We restructured the IA to make file access the fastest path through the product, surfacing recent, pending, and overdue documents at the top of every relevant view.

Decision 03

Design system as product foundation

We documented a full design system alongside the redesign, not as a separate deliverable, but as the actual source of truth for the new interface, grounded in real use cases rather than abstract principles.

Final solution

A platform that works for both sides of the relationship

Final design: file manager with clear status indicators and role-appropriate actions

Outcome

Less friction, faster workflows

The redesign significantly reduced the time accountants spent locating and managing client files. Client onboarding friction dropped as the simplified client view made it clearer what was expected of them and where things stood.

The design system gave the development team a shared language that had been missing, reducing design-development back-and-forth and making future feature additions more consistent by default.

Reflection

What designing for two users simultaneously taught me

When a product serves multiple roles, the temptation is to find the common ground and design for that. The better move is to understand where the roles diverge and design for both — different entry points, different defaults, different levels of detail, held together by a shared underlying system.

The design system work also reinforced something I've seen in every project: documentation done alongside design, not after, is categorically different. The system reflects the actual product decisions, not a post-hoc rationalisation of them.
