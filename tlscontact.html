01 — Context

A global operation running on a fragmented interface

TLScontact manages visa application centres across more than 90 countries, processing millions of visa applications on behalf of governments worldwide. The internal console is the operational backbone — used daily by processing staff, administrators, and operations managers to manage appointments, track applications, and coordinate cross-centre workflows.

At enterprise scale, interface inconsistency isn't an aesthetic problem — it's an operational risk. Fragmented flows mean longer training times, higher error rates, and slower operations. The redesign was a business-critical investment, not a cosmetic refresh.

02 — Problem

Years of accumulated inconsistency

Like many enterprise platforms that have grown over years, the console had accumulated significant structural debt. Different sections had been built by different teams at different times, resulting in inconsistent interaction patterns, fragmented information architecture, and visual noise that made it harder to distinguish what mattered.

The platform also had a significant accessibility gap. With diverse global users and legal obligations in multiple jurisdictions, WCAG 2.2 compliance wasn't optional — it was required.

Alongside the console, the business needed a native mobile app for appointment booking and a mobile-native version of the landing page — both requiring design work that extended the web design system to mobile contexts.

The scope

This is a live project with multiple parallel workstreams: the console redesign, native mobile app, landing page mobile conversion, WCAG 2.2 implementation, and full design system governance — all running concurrently in collaboration with stakeholders and engineering teams across different geographies.

03 — My role

Design ownership across the full product surface

I hold end-to-end design ownership across all product workstreams at TLScontact:

  • Designing the native mobile app (iOS/Android) for appointment booking
  • Leading the conversion of the responsive landing page to a dedicated native mobile product

04 — Design system

Building the foundation for everything that follows

One of the first decisions in the redesign was that the design system had to come first. Redesigning individual screens on top of an inconsistent foundation would produce inconsistent results. The design system needed to be the source of truth that all redesign work built on.

The system covers the full component library — from atomic elements (colour, typography, spacing, iconography) through to complex, product-specific components (data tables, form patterns, status indicators, navigation structures). Every component is documented, has defined states, and is built with accessibility baked in from the start.

The system extends to native mobile, maintaining conceptual consistency while respecting the distinct interaction conventions of iOS and Android. Components aren't copied from web — they're adapted to the mobile context while preserving the design language that ties both platforms together.

05 — Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 as a design constraint, not an afterthought

Accessibility was integrated into the design process from the start — not retrofitted at the end. This means colour contrast is tested at the token level, interactive elements have explicit focus states, form patterns include appropriate labelling and error handling, and the information architecture supports screen reader navigation.

The enterprise context adds complexity: users include people with a wide range of abilities, working in environments with different hardware, connectivity, and display conditions. Designing for this range isn't just a compliance exercise — it produces interfaces that work better for everyone.

What WCAG 2.2 implementation looks like in practice

Every new component in the design system goes through an accessibility review before it ships. Colour ratios, touch targets, keyboard navigation, focus order, ARIA patterns — these are checked in design, not delegated to engineering to figure out in implementation. The result is a system where accessibility is a property of the components, not an additional step.

06 — Mobile

Two platforms, one coherent product

The native mobile app focuses on appointment booking — one of the highest-volume, highest-frequency interactions in the visa application process. Designed for both iOS and Android, it follows platform conventions (HIG and Material Design respectively) while maintaining the visual language and interaction quality of the web console.

The landing page mobile conversion was a separate workstream — taking a responsive web experience and rebuilding it as a dedicated mobile product. The distinction matters: a responsive site adapts to mobile; a native app is built for mobile. The decisions about what to include, how to organise it, and how interactions should feel are fundamentally different.

07 — Collaboration

Design that works at enterprise scale

Enterprise product design is as much about stakeholder alignment as it is about the interface. The console serves users across dozens of countries, operated by teams with different workflows, different regulatory environments, and different levels of technical sophistication.

Working closely with stakeholders means translating those diverse operational realities into design decisions that work for the breadth of use cases without becoming so generic that they work well for none. And working closely with engineering means design decisions account for technical constraints from the start — not after the prototype has been built and the constraints surface during implementation.

08 — In progress

Still building. What I'm learning.

This is an ongoing project — the redesign is iterative, the design system is continuously evolving, and the mobile products are in active development. The challenge of enterprise product design at this scale is that you're never done; there's always more surface to improve and more complexity to resolve.

What I'm taking from this engagement is a sharper understanding of what design system governance actually requires at enterprise scale — not just components, but decision-making frameworks, contribution processes, and the discipline to keep a system coherent as the product and team grow.
